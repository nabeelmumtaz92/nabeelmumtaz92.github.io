UX Case Study · iOS + Watch App

RepTracker. The step counter for strength training.

Designing an app that turns gym reps into weekly gift-card rewards. Apple Watch sensors validate every rep automatically — three signals have to agree before a claim pays out — so lifters can earn real gift cards for work that can't be faked. Product spec, nine technical briefs, and a validation plan are done; designs are being built in Figma now.

The problem

45 million Americans lift weights. Nobody rewards them.

Walkers have Steppa, runners have Strava — but lifters have nothing. Step-counting reward apps work because steps auto-track and can't be faked. Strength training is different: reps require manual logging, and manual logging invites fraud.

Existing answers both miss. Video-proof apps add real friction — nobody films every set. Manual-logging apps just trust the honor system with money on the line. RepTracker is the gap: a frictionless, unhackable way for lifters to earn cash for their reps.

The approach

The watch is ground truth.

The design rests on a validation idea, not a form. The user does a workout and claims "45 bench reps." The Apple Watch's accelerometer already detected the rep motion — so the system checks three signals: accelerometer (did the watch detect 45 movement cycles?), physiology (is 45 reps realistic for the duration?), and heart rate (did HR elevate appropriately?). All three align → the entry is approved and the user qualifies for the weekly reward. Claim 100 while the watch saw 45 → rejected. It's impossible to fake all three at once.

That shifts the UX job: the app doesn't need to capture the workout, just to claim it in one tap and make the verdict trustworthy. The validation states — approved, needs review, rejected — are designed like the product itself, because when money is on the line, a confusing "why did my claim fail?" screen is a trust crisis, not an error message.

Designing as the engineer changes the designs. Every verdict the UI can show maps 1:1 to a backend validation state from the technical spec. The weekly goal, streak, and reward marketplace all run off the same rep ledger — nothing on screen is a mockup of data that doesn't exist yet.

The screens

Designs in progress — the core loop is mapped.

The Figma file is still early, so these are the flows being designed, not finished screens. Each one does exactly one job in the weekly habit loop.

01
🏠In progress

Home

Weekly goal progress (50 points — 30 reps = 1 point), current streak, and today's validation status at a glance. The week is the unit of the product — everything orients around it.

Design decisionWeekly goal, not daily. The affiliate reward pays out on the week's total, so the screen sells the weekly habit the business model needs.
02
✅In progress

Claim reps

One tap to claim the workout the watch already saw. The verdict comes back as approved, needs review, or rejected — with the reason stated plainly, never a code.

Design decisionThe workout already happened; claiming should cost seconds. Rejection states carry reasons because a lifter who doesn't understand "why" won't trust the next payout.
03
🎁In progress

Rewards marketplace

Hit 50 points for the week, then claim gift card rewards from brand partners (Optimum Nutrition, Rogue, Nike, Amazon) — points stack, so bigger totals unlock bigger payouts.

Design decisionRewards live one tap from Home, not buried in settings. The payout is the product's emotional peak; the design treats it that way.
04
📊In progress

Activity detail

Rep-by-rep breakdown of a validated workout: exercise classification, movement cycles, heart-rate curve. This is where "unhackable" becomes visible to the user.

Design decisionShow the evidence, not the algorithm. The user sees their own movement and heart rate — the same signals that approved the claim — which makes the verdict feel earned.

Design principles

Four rules the screens follow.

💰 Money on the line, plainly statedWhat the user earned and why is on the screen — no "pending" mysteries when a reward is involved.
🛡️ Fraud-proof by defaultValidation states are first-class designs, not error messages. The sensor verdict is the feature.
👆 One tap to claimThe workout already happened. Claiming it should take seconds, not a logging session.
⌚ Watch outranks phoneAccelerometer data settles disputes. The design never asks the user to prove what the sensor already knows.

What's next

Validation first — the spec defines what "working" means.

The test criteria set hard gates before scale: these are the numbers the MVP has to clear, and they're designed into the product (every verdict, streak, and redemption is measurable from day one).

  • Real-gym validation — rep counting accuracy >95% across 500 real workouts; >90% of cheating attempts caught MVP gate
  • Habit validation — D30 retention >35%; weekly goal achievement >45%; weekly redemption rate >70% MVP gate
  • Apple Watch MVP — HealthKit rep detection, exercise classification, 3-signal validation; Android + Samsung Watch after In progress
  • Premium tier — AI form feedback, 1RM estimates, programming, analytics at $9.99/mo Planned
  • B2B — coach white-label and gym challenges for retention and distribution Planned

Outcomes & context

A research-backed bet on an open window.

The product spec leans on published validation: rep counting from smartwatch accelerometers holds 0.92% error in research, and deep-learning exercise classification reaches 99.96% accuracy across 10 exercises. The reward model is Paceline's proven weekly-affiliate playbook ($29.5M Series A, 500K+ users) — applied to lifting, where no competitor exists yet.
